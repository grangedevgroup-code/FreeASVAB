# Mechanical Comprehension {data-sub="Forces, simple machines, gears, pulleys, levers, and fluids — the everyday physics of how things work"}

Mechanical Comprehension (MC) tests whether you understand **how machines and physical forces work**: levers, pulleys, gears, ramps, springs, fluids, and structures. Most questions include a **diagram**. On the computer test you get **15 questions in 22 minutes** — about a minute and a half each — so you have time to think each one through.

MC appears in many line scores: the Army's **CO, FA, MM, OF, SC,** and **ST**; the Air Force **Mechanical** composite; the Marine Corps **GT** and **MM**; and Navy mechanical and nuclear ratings. A few simple rules unlock most MC questions, and this chapter teaches all of them.

## Force, Work, Power, and Mechanical Advantage

- A **force** is a push or a pull, measured in **pounds** (lb) or **newtons** (N). **Weight** is the force of gravity on an object.
- **Work** is done when a force moves something **in the direction of the force**.

::: formula Work and power
**Work = Force × Distance**   (foot-pounds, ft-lb)
Lifting a 50-pound box 4 feet onto a shelf takes 50 × 4 = **200 ft-lb** of work.

**Power = Work ÷ Time**   (foot-pounds per second, or watts)
**1 horsepower = 550 ft-lb per second** (about 746 watts).
:::

- **Mechanical advantage (MA)** tells you how much a machine multiplies your force:

::: formula Mechanical advantage
**MA = Output force (load) ÷ Input force (effort)**

For an ideal machine (no friction), **MA = Effort distance ÷ Load distance**.
:::

::: key The golden rule of machines
**A machine can multiply force, but you pay for it with distance.** If a machine lets you lift 4 times as much weight, you must move your end 4 times as far. The work you put in (force × distance) is **at least as much as the work you get out** — no machine creates extra work. Friction makes every real machine lose a little, so its **efficiency** (work out ÷ work in) is below 100%.
:::

## Levers

A lever is a rigid bar that pivots on a point called the **fulcrum**. You push on one part (the **effort**) to move a **load** (the resistance).

::: formula The law of the lever
**Effort × Effort distance = Load × Load distance**

(Each distance is measured from the **fulcrum**.)
**MA of a lever = Effort distance ÷ Load distance**
:::

::: example Example: moving a heavy load
A 200-pound load sits 2 feet from the fulcrum. You push down 8 feet from the fulcrum on the other side. How much force do you need?
Effort × 8 = 200 × 2 → Effort × 8 = 400 → **Effort = 50 pounds**. The MA is 8 ÷ 2 = 4.
:::

### The three classes of levers

{{fig:mc-levers|The three classes of levers}}

| Class | What is in the middle | Examples | Force advantage? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First class** | The **fulcrum** (between effort and load) | Seesaw, crowbar, pry bar, scissors, pliers | Can multiply force or distance, depending on where the fulcrum is |
| **Second class** | The **load** (between fulcrum and effort) | **Wheelbarrow**, nutcracker, bottle opener, door | **Always** multiplies force (MA greater than 1) |
| **Third class** | The **effort** (between fulcrum and load) | Tweezers, broom, fishing rod, **your forearm** | MA **less than 1**: you lose force but gain speed and distance |
{.compact}

::: memory "F-L-E, 1-2-3"
Ask what is in the **middle**: **F**ulcrum → class **1**; **L**oad → class **2**; **E**ffort → class **3**.
:::

::: example A seesaw puzzle
A 60-pound child sits 5 feet from the center of a seesaw. Where must a 100-pound child sit to balance it?
60 × 5 = 100 × d → 300 = 100d → **d = 3 feet**. The **heavier** person sits **closer** to the fulcrum.
:::

## Pulleys

A pulley is a wheel with a grooved rim that guides a rope.

{{fig:mc-pulleys|Fixed pulley, movable pulley, and block and tackle}}

- A **fixed pulley** is attached to a support. It **changes the direction** of the force (you pull down to lift up), but it gives **no** force advantage: **MA = 1**.
- A **movable pulley** is attached to the load and moves with it. Two rope sections hold the load, so **MA = 2**: you need half the force, but you must pull twice as much rope.
- A **block and tackle** combines fixed and movable pulleys.

::: key The pulley shortcut
**MA = the number of rope sections that hold up the movable pulley (the load).**
Count the strands that go to the moving block. Do **not** count the strand you pull on if it only runs over a fixed pulley and pulls downward.
:::

::: example Example
A block and tackle has **4 rope sections** supporting a 200-pound load. The effort needed is 200 ÷ 4 = **50 pounds**. To raise the load 1 foot, you must pull **4 feet** of rope.
:::

## Wheel and Axle

A wheel and axle is a large wheel attached to a smaller axle so that they turn together: a steering wheel, a doorknob, a screwdriver handle, a winch crank.

{{fig:mc-wheel-axle|Wheel and axle: effort on the large wheel, load on the small axle}}

::: formula Wheel and axle
**MA = Radius of the wheel ÷ Radius of the axle**
(When the effort turns the wheel.)
:::

::: example Example
A winch has a crank (wheel) radius of 15 inches and an axle radius of 3 inches. MA = 15 ÷ 3 = 5, so lifting a 300-pound load takes 300 ÷ 5 = **60 pounds** of effort.
:::

If the force is applied to the **axle** instead (as an engine does to a car's drive axle), the machine trades force for **speed**: the rim of the wheel moves faster and farther than the axle.

## Inclined Planes, Wedges, and Screws

{{fig:mc-incline|An inclined plane trades a longer distance for a smaller force}}

::: formula Inclined plane (ramp)
**MA = Length of the slope ÷ Height**
Effort (ignoring friction) = Load × [[Height/Length]]
:::

::: example Example
Pushing a 400-pound barrel up a 12-foot ramp to a platform 3 feet high: MA = 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so the effort is 400 ÷ 4 = **100 pounds**. A **longer, gentler** ramp means **less force** but **more distance**.
:::

- A **wedge** is an inclined plane that moves: an axe, a knife, a chisel, a doorstop. A **longer, thinner** wedge has a **greater** mechanical advantage.
- A **screw** is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Each full turn moves the screw forward by its **pitch** (the distance between threads). A screw with **8 threads per inch** advances [[1/8]] inch per turn, so it takes 16 turns to move 2 inches. **Finer threads** = more turns = **more mechanical advantage**.

::: formula Screw jack
**MA = Distance the handle travels in one turn ÷ Pitch = 2π × handle length ÷ pitch**
A jack with a 14-inch handle and a [[1/4]]-inch pitch: the handle travels 2 × [[22/7]] × 14 = 88 inches per turn. MA = 88 ÷ [[1/4]] = **352**.
:::

## Gears

Gears are toothed wheels that transmit rotation from one shaft to another.

{{fig:mc-gears|Meshing gears turn in opposite directions; the smaller gear turns faster.}}

::: key Gear direction
- Two gears that **mesh** turn in **opposite directions**.
- In a line of meshing gears, they **alternate**: gears 1, 3, 5... turn the same way as the first gear; gears 2, 4, 6... turn the opposite way.
- An **idler gear** placed between two gears makes them turn the **same** direction, without changing their speed ratio.
- Gears fixed on the **same shaft** turn in the **same direction at the same speed**.
:::

::: formula Gear speed
**Teeth on gear A × rpm of gear A = Teeth on gear B × rpm of gear B**

The gear with **fewer teeth turns faster**. Example: a 20-tooth gear turning at 60 rpm drives a 40-tooth gear. 20 × 60 = 40 × N → **N = 30 rpm**.
:::

- **Speed vs. torque:** when a small gear drives a larger gear, the larger gear turns **slower** but with **more torque** (turning force). That is why low gears help a truck climb a hill. When a large gear drives a smaller one, you get more **speed** and less torque.
- **Gear ratio** = teeth on the driven gear ÷ teeth on the driver. A 12-tooth gear driving a 36-tooth gear has a 3:1 ratio: the output turns at one-third the speed with three times the torque.

| Gear type | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Spur gears** | Straight teeth; connect parallel shafts |
| **Bevel gears** | Cone-shaped; connect shafts at an angle (usually 90°), as in a hand drill or a car's differential |
| **Worm gear** | A screw-like worm turns a gear wheel; gives a **large speed reduction** and usually cannot be turned backward from the wheel |
| **Rack and pinion** | A gear (pinion) moves a straight toothed bar (rack); changes rotation into **straight-line** motion, as in car steering |
| **Planetary gears** | Gears that orbit a central gear; used in automatic transmissions |
{.compact}

## Belts and Chains

Belts and chains connect pulleys (sheaves) or sprockets that are some distance apart.

{{fig:mc-belts|An open belt keeps the same direction; a crossed belt reverses it.}}

- With an **open (uncrossed) belt**, both pulleys turn in the **same direction**.
- With a **crossed belt**, the pulleys turn in **opposite directions**.
- A chain on sprockets (like a bicycle's) works like an open belt: both sprockets turn the same direction.

::: formula Belt and pulley speed
**Diameter of A × rpm of A = Diameter of B × rpm of B**
A 4-inch pulley turning at 900 rpm drives a 12-inch pulley: 4 × 900 = 12 × N → **N = 300 rpm**. The **smaller** pulley always turns **faster**.
:::

## Cams, Cranks, and Other Mechanisms

| Device | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Cam** | A shaped wheel that turns rotation into **up-and-down** (back-and-forth) motion of a **follower**, such as an engine's valves. The follower rises once for **each lobe** on the cam per turn. |
| **Crank** | Converts rotation into back-and-forth motion, or the reverse (a crankshaft, a bicycle pedal) |
| **Ratchet and pawl** | Lets a wheel turn in **one direction only** (socket wrenches, winches) |
| **Universal joint** | Transfers rotation between two shafts that meet at an angle (a car's driveshaft) |
| **Clutch** | Connects and disconnects two rotating shafts |
| **Flywheel** | A heavy wheel that stores rotational energy and smooths out speed |
| **Linkage** | Connected bars that change the direction or size of a motion (a car's windshield wiper arms) |
{.compact}

## Springs

::: formula Hooke's law
**Force = k × stretch**
A spring stretches in **proportion** to the force on it (until it is overloaded). If 12 pounds stretches a spring 3 inches, it takes 4 pounds per inch, so 20 pounds stretches it **5 inches**.
:::

- **Springs in series** (one hanging from another): each spring carries the **full** load, so the stretches **add up**. The combination is **softer**.
- **Springs in parallel** (side by side, sharing the load): each spring carries **part** of the load, so the combination stretches **less**. It is **stiffer**.

::: example Example
A spring stretches 1 inch for every 5 pounds. Two of these springs side by side support a 20-pound weight. Each spring carries 10 pounds, so each (and the weight) moves down **2 inches**. Hung one below the other, the same 20 pounds would stretch each spring 4 inches — **8 inches** in all.
:::

## Fluids: Pressure, Hydraulics, Buoyancy, and Flow

::: formula Pressure
**Pressure = Force ÷ Area**   (pounds per square inch, psi)
150 pounds spread over 30 square inches = 150 ÷ 30 = **5 psi**.
:::

### Hydraulics

**Pascal's principle:** pressure applied to a confined liquid is passed **equally in all directions** throughout the liquid. Because liquids barely compress, a hydraulic system can multiply force.

{{fig:mc-hydraulic|A hydraulic press: a small force on a small piston makes a large force on a large piston}}

::: example Example: hydraulic jack
A 50-pound force pushes on a small piston with an area of 2 square inches. That makes a pressure of 50 ÷ 2 = 25 psi. The large piston has an area of 40 square inches, so it pushes up with 25 × 40 = **1,000 pounds**.
The trade-off: the small piston must move **20 times as far** as the large piston rises (40 ÷ 2 = 20).
:::

### Pressure in liquids

- Pressure in a liquid **increases with depth** (about 0.43 psi for every foot of fresh water).
- Pressure at a given depth depends **only on the depth**, not on the shape or width of the container.
- Water "seeks its own level": connected containers fill to the same height.

### Buoyancy

**Archimedes' principle:** a liquid pushes up on an object with a force equal to the **weight of the liquid the object pushes aside** (displaces). An object **floats** if it can displace its own weight of liquid before it is completely underwater; otherwise it **sinks**.

### Flowing fluids

- In a pipe, the same amount of fluid must pass every point each second, so where the pipe **narrows**, the fluid moves **faster**.
- **Bernoulli's principle:** where a fluid moves **faster**, its pressure is **lower**. Air moving faster over the curved top of a wing helps create **lift**.
- Heating a gas in a sealed container **raises its pressure**; squeezing a gas into a smaller space raises its pressure and temperature.

| Valve | Job |
|---|---|
| **Check valve** | Lets fluid flow in **one direction only** |
| **Gate valve** | Fully opens or closes a line |
| **Globe or needle valve** | Adjusts the rate of flow precisely |
| **Relief (safety) valve** | Opens automatically when pressure gets too high |
{.compact}

## Structures, Stability, and Center of Gravity

- An object's **center of gravity** is the point where its weight seems to be concentrated. An object is **stable** when its center of gravity is **low** and its **base is wide**. It tips over when its center of gravity moves outside its base.
- A **triangle** is the only simple shape that cannot change shape without bending or breaking a side, which is why bridges, towers, and roof trusses are built from triangles. A diagonal brace makes a rectangular frame rigid.
- Materials are loaded in **tension** (pulled), **compression** (squeezed), **shear** (one part sliding past another), **torsion** (twisted), and **bending**. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension; steel is strong in tension.

### Weight on two supports

When a weight rests on a beam held up by two supports, the support **closer** to the weight carries **more** of it.

{{fig:mc-beam|The support nearer the load carries more of the weight.}}

::: example Example
A 10-foot beam rests on supports at each end. A 120-pound weight sits 2 feet from the left support (and 8 feet from the right one). Ignoring the beam's own weight:
- Left support: 120 × [[8/10]] = **96 pounds**
- Right support: 120 × [[2/10]] = **24 pounds**

Notice the trick: each support's share uses the distance to the **other** support. The two shares add up to the full 120 pounds.
:::

## Heat and Materials

- Most materials **expand when heated** and contract when cooled. Different metals expand by different amounts.
- A **bimetallic strip** bonds two different metals together. When heated, the metal that expands **more** ends up on the **outside** of the curve, so the strip **bends toward the metal that expands less**. Thermostats and some switches use this.
- Metal lids loosen under hot water because the metal expands more than the glass jar.

## Motion

- **Rotating objects:** every point on a spinning wheel makes one turn in the same time, but points **farther from the center travel farther**, so they move **faster**.
- **Pendulums:** a pendulum's swing time depends on its **length**, not its weight. A **longer** pendulum swings **more slowly**.
- An object moving in a circle tends to fly off in a **straight line** (inertia); a force pointing toward the center (**centripetal force**) keeps it in the circle.
- **Friction** opposes motion. It is greater between rough surfaces and when objects are pressed together harder. **Rolling** friction (wheels, ball bearings) is much less than **sliding** friction, and **lubrication** reduces friction.
- An object thrown straight out sideways and an object dropped from the same height **hit the ground at the same time** — gravity pulls both down at the same rate.

## MC Problem-Solving Tips

- **Write down the rule** first (MA = ..., E × d = L × d), then plug in the numbers.
- **Gears and belts:** draw a small arrow on each gear in the diagram, alternating direction for each mesh, and check whether belts are crossed.
- **Pulleys:** count only the strands that **support the moving load**.
- **Check the trade-off:** if your answer says a machine reduces force, make sure the effort moves **farther** than the load.
- **Think about real life:** would you rather push a heavy cart up a long gentle ramp or a short steep one? Would you grip a long wrench near the end or near the nut? Intuition is usually right — the formulas just put numbers on it.
