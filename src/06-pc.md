# Paragraph Comprehension {data-sub="Read short passages quickly, find the proof, and dodge the trap answers"}

Paragraph Comprehension (PC) gives you a short passage — usually one paragraph — and asks one or more questions about it. Together with Word Knowledge, it makes up your **verbal score (VE)**, which counts **double** in the AFQT.

Here is the best news in this chapter: **every answer is in the passage.** You never need outside knowledge. Your job is to find the proof. On the computer test you have about **2 minutes 40 seconds per question** — plenty of time to read carefully and check your answer against the text.

## The Six Question Types

Almost every PC question is one of these six types. Learn to recognize them and you will know exactly where to look.

| Type | How the question is worded | Where the answer is |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Main idea** | "The passage is mainly about..." "The best title would be..." "The main point is..." | The whole passage, especially the first and last sentences |
| **2. Stated detail** | "According to the passage..." "The passage states that..." | One specific sentence — find it and match it |
| **3. Inference** | "It can be inferred that..." "The passage suggests..." "The author would most likely agree..." | Not stated directly, but clearly supported by the facts given |
| **4. Vocabulary in context** | "As used in the passage, *X* most nearly means..." | The sentence containing the word |
| **5. Purpose and tone** | "The author's purpose is to..." "The tone of the passage is..." | The overall approach: informing, persuading, warning, describing, entertaining |
| **6. Sequence or cause** | "What should be done first?" "What caused...?" "Which happened last?" | Time words (*first, then, after*) and cause words (*because, so, as a result*) |

## The Five-Step Method

<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Read the question first.</strong> Know what you are hunting for before you read the passage.</li>
<li><strong>Read the passage actively.</strong> Ask yourself: What is the topic? What is the author's main point? Notice signal words like <em>however</em> and <em>because</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Predict the answer in your own words</strong> before looking at the choices.</li>
<li><strong>Find the proof.</strong> Point to the sentence in the passage that supports your answer. If you cannot point to it, be suspicious.</li>
<li><strong>Eliminate the traps</strong> (below), then choose.</li>
</ol>

::: key The golden rule of Paragraph Comprehension
The correct answer is the one the **passage supports** — not the one that is true in real life, and not the one you agree with. If a choice sounds true but the passage never says or implies it, it is wrong.
:::

## The Six Trap Answers

Wrong answers on PC questions are not random. They fall into predictable patterns.

| Trap | What it looks like | How to beat it |
|---|---|---|
| **Outside knowledge** | A true fact that the passage never mentions | Ask: "Where does the passage say this?" |
| **Too broad** | A main-idea choice that covers far more than the passage does | The main idea should match the passage's actual scope |
| **Too narrow** | A main-idea choice that covers only one detail | A detail is not the main idea |
| **Extreme language** | *always, never, all, none, only, completely* | Passages rarely make absolute claims; prefer moderate choices |
| **Opposite** | Uses the passage's words but reverses the meaning | Reread the exact sentence, including *not* and *except* |
| **Twisted details** | Mixes real words from the passage into a false statement | Check that every part of the choice is correct |
{.compact}

## Signal Words: The Road Signs of a Passage

| Signal | Words | What they tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast** | however, but, although, yet, despite, on the other hand, instead | A change of direction — what follows is often the author's real point |
| **Continuation** | also, in addition, furthermore, moreover, likewise | More of the same idea |
| **Cause and effect** | because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, so | One thing leads to another |
| **Example** | for example, for instance, such as | A specific case that supports a general point |
| **Sequence** | first, next, then, before, after, finally | Order of steps or events |
| **Conclusion** | in conclusion, overall, thus, in short | The author is summing up the main point |
{.compact}

## Worked Example: All Six Types in One Passage

> Before the 1940s, most military aircraft were built from wood and fabric. These materials were light and easy to repair, but they could not withstand the stresses of higher speeds. As engines became more powerful, designers turned to aluminum, which was strong yet light. Today, many advanced aircraft use composite materials made of carbon fibers bonded with resin. Composites are even lighter than aluminum and resist corrosion, though they can be expensive to manufacture and difficult to repair in the field.

::: example 1. Main idea
**The passage is mainly about:** how the materials used to build military aircraft have changed over time.

Why not "the advantages of composite materials"? That is **too narrow** — composites are only the last part of the passage. Why not "the history of military aviation"? That is **too broad** — the passage discusses only building materials.
:::

::: example 2. Stated detail
**According to the passage, one advantage of composites is that they:** resist corrosion.

The proof is in the last sentence: "Composites are even lighter than aluminum and resist corrosion."
:::

::: example 3. Inference
**It can be inferred that wood and fabric were replaced mainly because:** they could not handle the stresses of faster aircraft.

The passage never says "wood was replaced because..." but it says wood and fabric "could not withstand the stresses of higher speeds" and that designers turned to aluminum "as engines became more powerful." Put those together and the inference is clear.
:::

::: example 4. Vocabulary in context
**As used in the passage, "withstand" most nearly means:** resist or endure.

Test it in the sentence: "they could not *resist* the stresses of higher speeds." It fits.
:::

::: example 5. Purpose and tone
**The author's main purpose is to:** inform — to explain how aircraft materials developed. The tone is neutral and factual. There is no argument (persuade) and no story (entertain).
:::

::: example 6. Sequence
**Which material came into wide use first?** Wood and fabric, then aluminum, then composites. Time words ("Before the 1940s," "As engines became more powerful," "Today") show the order.
:::

## Getting Better at Reading (for the Long Term)

- **Read nonfiction 15 minutes a day:** news articles, science magazines, history, biographies, manuals.
- **Summarize each paragraph in one short sentence** as you read. This trains you to find main ideas automatically.
- **Look for the "however."** Authors often state a common belief and then correct it. The correction is usually the point.
- **Build vocabulary** (Chapter 5). Unknown words are the number-one reason people misread passages.

::: tip When two answers look right
Go back to the passage and compare the two choices word by word. One of them almost always contains a small word — *only, always, never, most, some* — that the passage does not support. That is your wrong answer.
:::
