# Electronics Information {data-sub="Current, voltage, resistance, circuits, and components — with Ohm's law made easy"}

Electronics Information (EI) tests basic electricity and electronics: what current and voltage are, how circuits behave, what common parts do, and how to read simple diagrams. On the computer test you get **15 questions in 10 minutes**, so this is mostly about **knowing the facts and a few formulas**.

EI is not part of the AFQT, but it feeds **electronics** line scores in every branch — Army **EL** and **GM**, Air Force **E**, Marine **EL**, and Navy and Coast Guard technical ratings. If you want a job in electronics, IT, cyber, radar, avionics, or communications, EI is worth real study time.

## The Big Four: Current, Voltage, Resistance, Power

The easiest way to understand electricity is to compare it with **water flowing through a hose**.

| Quantity | What it is | Water comparison | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Voltage** (electromotive force, EMF) | The electrical "pressure" that pushes electrons | Water pressure | **volt (V)** | E or V |
| **Current** | The flow of electrons through a circuit | Amount of water flowing per second | **ampere, or amp (A)** | I |
| **Resistance** | Opposition to the flow of current | A narrow or clogged hose | **ohm (Ω)** | R |
| **Power** | The rate at which electrical energy is used | How much work the water can do | **watt (W)** | P |
{.compact}

{{fig:ei-water|The water analogy: voltage pushes, current flows, resistance restricts}}

::: memory The current's symbol is I
Current uses the letter **I** (from the French *intensité*). Voltage can be written **E** (for electromotive force) or **V**.
:::

- Current is measured with an **ammeter** (connected *in series*). Voltage is measured with a **voltmeter** (connected *in parallel*, across the part). Resistance is measured with an **ohmmeter** (with the power **off**). A **multimeter** does all three.
- **Conductors** let current flow easily (copper, silver, gold, aluminum). **Silver** is the best conductor; **copper** is the most common wiring material. **Insulators** block current (rubber, plastic, glass, ceramic, dry wood).
- **Semiconductors** (silicon, germanium) are in between. Their conductivity can be controlled, which makes diodes, transistors, and computer chips possible.

## Ohm's Law

::: formula Ohm's law
**E = I × R**   (Voltage = Current × Resistance)

Rearranged: **I = [[E/R]]** and **R = [[E/I]]**
:::

{{fig:ei-ohm-triangle|Cover the letter you want; the other two show you how to find it.}}

::: example Examples
- A 12-volt battery is connected to a 4-ohm resistor. Current: I = [[12/4]] = **3 amps**.
- 2 amps flow through a 50-ohm resistor. Voltage: E = 2 × 50 = **100 volts**.
- A 120-volt circuit draws 0.5 amp. Resistance: R = [[120/0.5]] = **240 ohms**.
:::

::: key How the three are related
- **More voltage → more current** (if resistance stays the same).
- **More resistance → less current** (if voltage stays the same).
:::

## Power

::: formula The power formula
**P = E × I**   (Power in watts = Volts × Amps)

Combined with Ohm's law, you can also write **P = I^2 × R** and **P = [[E^2/R]]**.
:::

::: example Examples
- A 120-volt appliance draws 10 amps: P = 120 × 10 = **1,200 watts**.
- A 60-watt bulb on 120 volts draws I = [[60/120]] = **0.5 amp**.
- 3 amps through a 10-ohm resistor: P = 3^2 × 10 = **90 watts**.
:::

**Energy use** is power × time, measured in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**. A 1,000-watt heater running for 3 hours uses 3 kWh.

::: memory "Twinkle, twinkle, little star — power equals I squared R"
Also remember **"PIE"**: **P** = **I** × **E**.
:::

## Series and Parallel Circuits

A **circuit** is a complete loop that current can flow through: a **source** (battery or generator), a **load** (something that uses power — a lamp, a motor), and **conductors** (wires), often with a **switch** and a **fuse**.

- A **closed circuit** is complete, and current flows.
- An **open circuit** has a break (an open switch, a cut wire, a burned-out bulb), and **no current** flows.
- A **short circuit** is an unintended low-resistance path. Current becomes dangerously high, which can cause heat, fire, or a blown fuse.

{{fig:ei-series-parallel|Series: one path. Parallel: several branches.}}

| | **Series circuit** | **Parallel circuit** |
|---|---|---|
| Paths for current | **One** path | **Two or more** branches |
| Current | **The same** through every part | Splits among the branches; total = sum of branch currents |
| Voltage | **Divides** among the parts; the drops add up to the source voltage | **The same** across every branch |
| Total resistance | **Add them up:** R~{T} = R~{1} + R~{2} + R~{3}... | **Less than the smallest** branch resistance |
| If one part fails (opens) | **Everything stops** (old-style holiday lights) | The other branches **keep working** (house wiring) |
{.compact}

::: formula Total resistance
**Series:** R~{T} = R~{1} + R~{2} + R~{3} + ...

**Parallel, two resistors:** R~{T} = [[(R~{1} × R~{2})/(R~{1} + R~{2})]]   ("product over sum")

**Parallel, any number:** [[1/R~{T}]] = [[1/R~{1}]] + [[1/R~{2}]] + [[1/R~{3}]] + ...

**Parallel, equal resistors:** R~{T} = one resistor's value ÷ the number of resistors
:::

::: example Examples
- **Series:** 10 Ω + 20 Ω + 30 Ω = **60 Ω**. On a 120-volt source, the current is [[120/60]] = 2 amps everywhere in the loop.
- **Parallel:** 6 Ω and 3 Ω → [[(6 × 3)/(6 + 3)]] = [[18/9]] = **2 Ω**.
- **Parallel, equal:** four 100-Ω resistors in parallel → [[100/4]] = **25 Ω**.
:::

::: tip A quick check for parallel resistance
The total resistance of a parallel circuit is **always smaller than the smallest branch**. Adding more branches gives current more paths, so total resistance goes **down** and total current goes **up**.
:::

### Batteries in series and parallel

- **Batteries in series** (positive to negative): the **voltages add**. Four 1.5-volt cells in series = **6 volts**.
- **Batteries in parallel** (positive to positive): the **voltage stays the same**, but the batteries can supply current longer.

## AC and DC

| | Direct current (DC) | Alternating current (AC) |
|---|---|---|
| Flow | One direction only | Reverses direction many times a second |
| Sources | Batteries, solar cells | Wall outlets, generators (alternators) |
| U.S. household standard | — | **120 volts at 60 hertz** (240 volts for large appliances) |
{.compact}

- **Frequency** is the number of complete cycles per second, measured in **hertz (Hz)**. 1 kilohertz (kHz) = 1,000 Hz; 1 megahertz (MHz) = 1,000,000 Hz.
- A **rectifier** (made of diodes) converts **AC to DC**. An **inverter** converts **DC to AC**.
- **Transformers** work only with **AC**.

## Electronic Components

{{fig:ei-symbols|Common schematic symbols}}

| Component | What it does | Unit / key fact |
|---|---|---|
| **Resistor** | Limits (resists) current | Ohms (Ω); value shown by color bands |
| **Capacitor** | **Stores** electric charge and releases it; **blocks DC and passes AC**; smooths power supplies | **Farads (F)**, usually microfarads (µF) |
| **Inductor (coil)** | Stores energy in a magnetic field; **opposes changes in current**; passes DC, resists AC | **Henrys (H)** |
| **Transformer** | **Steps AC voltage up or down** using two coils | Works only with AC |
| **Diode** | Lets current flow in **one direction only** | Used in rectifiers |
| **LED** (light-emitting diode) | A diode that gives off light | Has polarity: the longer leg is positive |
| **Transistor** | Acts as a **switch** or an **amplifier**; three leads: emitter, base, collector | NPN and PNP types |
| **Integrated circuit (IC)** | Many transistors and other parts on one chip | Found in computers and phones |
| **Fuse** | **Melts** to open the circuit when current is too high; must be replaced | Rated in amps |
| **Circuit breaker** | **Trips** to open the circuit when current is too high; can be **reset** | Rated in amps |
| **Switch** | Opens or closes a circuit by hand | SPST, SPDT, DPDT |
| **Relay** | An electrically operated switch (a small current controls a larger one) | Uses an electromagnet |
| **Potentiometer / rheostat** | A variable resistor (volume knob, dimmer) | Ohms |
| **Ground** | Connection to earth or a common return path; a safety path for fault current | Symbol: stacked lines |
{.compact}

::: key Capacitors vs. inductors
- A **capacitor** stores energy in an **electric field**, **blocks DC**, and **passes AC**. Its opposition to AC (capacitive reactance) goes **down** as frequency goes **up**.
- An **inductor** stores energy in a **magnetic field**, **passes DC**, and **opposes AC**. Its opposition to AC (inductive reactance) goes **up** as frequency goes **up**.
- The total opposition to current in an AC circuit is called **impedance** (also measured in ohms).
:::

### Transformers

A transformer has a **primary** coil (input) and a **secondary** coil (output) wound on an iron core.

::: formula Transformer ratio
[[V~{secondary}/V~{primary}]] = [[N~{secondary}/N~{primary}]]   (N = number of turns of wire)

- **More turns** on the secondary → **step-up** transformer (higher voltage out).
- **Fewer turns** on the secondary → **step-down** transformer (lower voltage out).
- Example: 120 V in, 200 turns primary, 20 turns secondary → 120 × [[20/200]] = **12 V** out.
:::

### Diodes and transistors

- A **diode** has an **anode** (+) and a **cathode** (−, marked with a band). Current flows from anode to cathode when **forward-biased** and is blocked when **reverse-biased**.
- A **transistor** uses a small current at its **base** to control a larger current between its **collector** and **emitter**. That lets it work as an electronic **switch** (in computers) or an **amplifier** (in radios and speakers).

## The Resistor Color Code

Most small resistors show their value with colored bands. The first two bands are **digits**, the third band is the **multiplier** (number of zeros), and a fourth band shows the **tolerance**.

| Color | Digit | Multiplier | | Color | Digit | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Black** | 0 | × 1 | | **Green** | 5 | × 100,000 |
| **Brown** | 1 | × 10 | | **Blue** | 6 | × 1,000,000 |
| **Red** | 2 | × 100 | | **Violet** | 7 | × 10,000,000 |
| **Orange** | 3 | × 1,000 | | **Gray** | 8 | — |
| **Yellow** | 4 | × 10,000 | | **White** | 9 | — |
{.compact}

**Tolerance band:** **gold = ±5%**, **silver = ±10%**, no band = ±20%.

::: memory Remember the color order: 0 to 9
**B**ig **B**oys **R**ace **O**ur **Y**oung **G**irls, **B**ut **V**iolet **G**enerally **W**ins
**B**lack 0, **B**rown 1, **R**ed 2, **O**range 3, **Y**ellow 4, **G**reen 5, **B**lue 6, **V**iolet 7, **G**ray 8, **W**hite 9.
:::

{{fig:ei-resistor|Reading a resistor: yellow, violet, red, gold = 4, 7, × 100, ±5% = 4,700 Ω (4.7 kΩ)}}

::: example More examples
- **Brown, black, red:** 1, 0, × 100 = **1,000 Ω** (1 kΩ)
- **Red, red, brown:** 2, 2, × 10 = **220 Ω**
- **Orange, orange, orange:** 3, 3, × 1,000 = **33,000 Ω** (33 kΩ)
- **Brown, black, green:** 1, 0, × 100,000 = **1,000,000 Ω** (1 MΩ)
:::

## Wires, Magnetism, and Generators

- **Wire gauge (AWG):** the **smaller the gauge number, the thicker the wire**. Thicker wire has **less resistance** and can safely carry **more current**. Typical house wiring is 14-gauge (15-amp circuits) and 12-gauge (20-amp circuits).
- A wire's resistance goes **up** as it gets **longer** or **thinner**, and usually as it gets **hotter**.
- Current flowing through a wire creates a **magnetic field** around it. Wrapping the wire into a coil around an iron core makes an **electromagnet**.
- **Electromagnetic induction:** moving a magnet near a coil (or a coil near a magnet) **produces current**. This is how **generators** work. **Motors** do the reverse: they use current and magnetism to produce **motion**.
- In a car, the **alternator** is a generator that produces AC, which diodes convert to DC to charge the battery.

## Radio, Signals, and Antennas

- Radio waves are electromagnetic waves. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
- **AM** (amplitude modulation) varies the **strength** of the carrier wave; **FM** (frequency modulation) varies its **frequency**. FM has less static.
- An **antenna** sends or receives radio waves. A **speaker** turns electrical signals into sound; a **microphone** turns sound into electrical signals.
- **Analog** signals vary continuously; **digital** signals use separate on/off values (1s and 0s).

## Electrical Safety

- **Always turn off and lock out power** before working on a circuit.
- A **GFCI** (ground-fault circuit interrupter) outlet cuts power in a fraction of a second if current leaks to ground — required near water (kitchens, bathrooms, outdoors).
- The **ground** wire (green or bare) carries fault current safely away. In U.S. house wiring, the **hot** wire is usually **black** (or red), the **neutral** is **white**, and the **ground** is **green or bare copper**.
- **Never replace a fuse with a higher-rated one** — the wire could overheat and start a fire.
- It is **current** through the body that injures. As little as a tenth of an amp through the chest can be fatal, and wet skin lowers your body's resistance.
- Discharge large **capacitors** before touching them; they can hold a dangerous charge even after power is off.
