# Test-Day Checklists {.nonum data-label="Quick Reference" data-sub="What to do in the last week, the night before, and on test day"}

## One Week Before

- Take your last full practice test (Practice Test 2) under real timing.
- Review your **error log** and redo every question you missed.
- Memorize the **Formula and Fact Sheet**. Write it from memory once a day.
- Keep up your daily vocabulary review — but stop adding brand-new words two days before the test.
- Confirm your **test date, time, and location** with your recruiter, and ask what to bring.

## The Night Before

- Do a **light** review only (30 minutes or less): formulas, word parts, and your error log.
- Lay out your **ID** and any documents your recruiter told you to bring.
- Plan your route and travel time. If you are staying at a hotel for MEPS processing, follow your recruiter's instructions.
- Get to bed early. Aim for seven to nine hours of sleep.

## The Morning of the Test

- Eat a normal breakfast with some protein. Drink some water.
- Wear comfortable clothes in layers.
- Leave early — late arrivals are turned away.
- Use the restroom before testing starts.
- Remind yourself: *read carefully, predict, eliminate, check.*

## During the Test

- Read every question and every answer choice.
- **Computer test:** you cannot skip or go back, so confirm each answer before moving on. Hard questions mean you are doing well.
- **Paper test:** answer the easy questions first, come back to the hard ones, and never leave a bubble blank.
- Watch the clock, but do not rush. If time runs short on the computer test, keep reading and answering for real.
- If you feel stuck or anxious: breathe in for four counts, hold for four, breathe out for four, then reread the question.

## After the Test

- If you take the CAT-ASVAB at a MEPS or MET site, your scores are available right after the session. Ask your recruiter to explain your **AFQT** and **line scores**.
- Look up the line-score requirements for the jobs you want.
- If you plan to retest, remember the waiting periods (1 month, 1 month, then 6 months) and that your **most recent** score counts. Retest only when your practice scores are clearly higher.

# Glossary {.nonum data-label="Quick Reference" data-sub="ASVAB and enlistment terms in plain English"}

::: div vocab
**AFQT (Armed Forces Qualification Test)** — The score, from 1 to 99, that decides whether you can enlist. It is computed from Arithmetic Reasoning, Mathematics Knowledge, Word Knowledge, and Paragraph Comprehension.

**AFQT category** — A group of AFQT scores: I (93–99), II (65–92), IIIA (50–64), IIIB (31–49), IVA (21–30), IVB (16–20), IVC (10–15), V (1–9).

**APT (AFQT Predictor Test)** — A short, 20-question online test some recruiters use to predict your AFQT before scheduling the full ASVAB.

**AS (Auto and Shop)** — The combined score for Auto Information and Shop Information.

**ASVAB** — Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery, the enlistment test for all branches.

**CAT-ASVAB** — The computer adaptive version of the ASVAB, given at MEPS and most MET sites. Questions adjust in difficulty based on your answers.

**Composite score (line score)** — A combination of subtest scores that each branch uses to decide which jobs you qualify for, such as the Army's GT or the Air Force's MAGE scores.

**Confirmation test (C-Test)** — A test required when a retest score jumps 20 or more AFQT points within six months, to verify the gain.

**Equating** — The statistical process that makes scores mean the same thing on every version of the test, paper or computer.

**Future Soldier Preparatory Course** — An Army program (with an academic track for applicants scoring at least 21) that teaches ASVAB subjects and offers chances to retest.

**GT (General Technical)** — An important line score. In the Army it is VE + AR; a GT of 110 is a common requirement for programs such as Officer Candidate School.

**Item Response Theory (IRT)** — The scoring method the ASVAB uses. It accounts for each question's difficulty, so your score is not simply the number you got right.

**Line score** — See composite score.

**MAGE** — The Air Force and Space Force composites: Mechanical, Administrative, General, and Electronics.

**MEPS (Military Entrance Processing Station)** — A joint-service facility where applicants take the ASVAB, have medical exams, and complete enlistment processing. There are 65 MEPS across the United States and Puerto Rico.

**MET site (Military Entrance Test site)** — A satellite testing location, often in a federal building, National Guard armory, or Reserve center.

**P&P-ASVAB** — The paper-and-pencil version of the test, given at only a few MET sites.

**Percentile** — A score showing the percentage of a reference group that scored at or below you. An AFQT of 75 means you did as well as or better than 75% of the reference group.

**PiCAT (Pending Internet Computerized Adaptive Test)** — An unproctored version of the computer ASVAB you can take at home, once, if you have never taken the ASVAB. It must be confirmed by a proctored Verification Test.

**Rating** — A Navy or Coast Guard enlisted job (other services use terms such as MOS or AFSC).

**Score of record** — Your official score. For the ASVAB, it is your most recent valid score, not your highest.

**Standard score** — A subtest score on a scale where the average is 50 and most people score between 40 and 60.

**Student ASVAB** — The version given in high schools through the Career Exploration Program. It has no Assembling Objects subtest; its scores can be used to enlist for two years if verified.

**Tryout questions** — Unscored questions being tested for future use. On the CAT-ASVAB they are mixed into two to four subtests, and those subtests get extra time.

**VE (Verbal Expression)** — A verbal score built from Word Knowledge and Paragraph Comprehension. It counts double in the AFQT.

**Verification Test (Vtest)** — A short (25–30 minute) proctored test that confirms a PiCAT score. It must be taken within 45 days of the PiCAT.
:::

You now have everything you need: how the test works, how it is scored, the strategies that protect your points, and every subject explained from the ground up — plus hundreds of practice questions. A 70 to 80 AFQT is well within reach for anyone who studies a little every day and learns from every mistake. Keep going, trust your preparation, and walk into that testing room ready.
