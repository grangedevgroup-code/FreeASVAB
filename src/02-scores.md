# Your Scores: AFQT, Line Scores, and What 70–80 Means {data-sub="How the scoring works, what every branch requires, and how retesting works"}

You will get three kinds of scores from the ASVAB. Once you understand them, you will know exactly where your study time pays off the most.

| Score | What it is | What it is used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard scores** | One score for each subtest | The building blocks for everything else |
| **AFQT percentile** (1–99) | A combination of WK, PC, AR, and MK | Whether you can **enlist** |
| **Line scores / composites** | Combinations of subtests, different for each branch | Which **jobs** you qualify for |

## Standard Scores

Each subtest is reported as a **standard score**. Standard scores are set so that the **average is 50** and the **standard deviation is 10**, based on a national sample of young people. In plain English:

- About **half** of people score **50 or higher** on a subtest.
- Only about **16%** score **60 or higher**.
- A 70 is two steps (two standard deviations) above average — very strong.

You do not need to calculate any of this. Just know that every subtest score is a comparison with other people, not a percentage of questions you got right.

## How the AFQT Is Built

Your AFQT combines your standard scores from four subtests. First, your **Word Knowledge** and **Paragraph Comprehension** results are combined into one verbal score called **VE** (Verbal Expression). Then the commonly published formula is:

::: formula The AFQT formula
**AFQT = 2 × VE + AR + MK**

where VE comes from Word Knowledge and Paragraph Comprehension together, AR is Arithmetic Reasoning, and MK is Mathematics Knowledge.
:::

{{fig:afqt-pie|Verbal skills make up about half of the AFQT; each math subtest is about a quarter.}}

That total is then converted to a **percentile** from 1 to 99. Your percentile compares you with a nationally representative group of 18-to-23-year-olds who took the ASVAB in a 1997 norming study.

- An AFQT of **50** means you scored as well as or better than **50%** of that group.
- An AFQT of **75** means you scored as well as or better than **75%** of that group.
- The highest possible AFQT is **99**.

::: key What this means for your study plan
- **Verbal is half the AFQT.** Every vocabulary word you learn and every reading strategy you master is worth double.
- **Arithmetic Reasoning** is a quarter of the AFQT by itself, and it also appears in almost every job composite.
- The technical subtests (GS, EI, AS, MC, AO) **do not affect your AFQT at all** — but they matter a lot for which jobs you can choose.
:::

## AFQT Categories

The services group AFQT scores into categories. These come straight from the official ASVAB program.

| Category | AFQT range | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | 93–99 | Top of the scale |
| **II** | 65–92 | Well above average — **your 70–80 goal lives here** |
| **IIIA** | 50–64 | Above average |
| **IIIB** | 31–49 | Average to slightly below; the usual minimum to enlist is in this band |
| **IVA** | 21–30 | Below most enlistment minimums |
| **IVB** | 16–20 | |
| **IVC** | 10–15 | |
| **V** | 1–9 | Not eligible to enlist |

Federal law bars Category V applicants from enlisting and limits how many Category IV recruits the services can accept. In practice, every branch sets its minimum at about 31 or higher for applicants with a high school diploma.

## Minimum AFQT Scores by Branch

These are the published minimums for applicants with a **high school diploma**, as listed on official recruiting websites in 2026. Applicants with a **GED** or other alternative credential usually need **50 or higher**.

| Branch | High school diploma | GED / alternative credential | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Army** | 31 | Higher (commonly 50) | Applicants scoring at least 21 may qualify for the 90-day academic track of the **Future Soldier Preparatory Course**, which teaches ASVAB subjects and includes chances to retest. |
| **Navy** | 31 | Higher (commonly 50) | Many ratings (jobs) have their own line-score requirements. |
| **Air Force** | 31 | 50 | Applicants with neither a diploma nor a GED need **65**. |
| **Space Force** | About 31–36 (published figures vary) | Higher | Very competitive, highly technical jobs; strong scores are expected. |
| **Marine Corps** | 31 | 50 | GED applicants are commonly also asked for college credits. |
| **Coast Guard** | 32 | Higher (commonly 50) | Officer programs use different tests. |

::: trap Minimums move
Minimum scores and education rules change with each service's recruiting needs, and a service can require more than its minimum when it has plenty of applicants. Meeting the minimum also does **not** mean you qualify for the job you want. Always confirm current requirements with a recruiter.
:::

## What a 70–80 AFQT Gets You

An AFQT of 70 to 80 puts you solidly in **Category II** — ahead of 70 to 80 percent of the reference group. That score:

- Clears the minimum of **every branch** by a wide margin, including the stricter GED and no-credential minimums.
- Usually means your **verbal and math** subtest scores are strong — and those subtests appear in most line scores, so many more jobs open up.
- Makes you a more attractive applicant. Some enlistment incentives and special programs are reserved for higher-scoring applicants (often those in Category IIIA, 50+, or above). Ask your recruiter what is available for your score.

Remember: the AFQT gets you **in the door**. Your **line scores** pick the room. If you have a particular job in mind, find out which subtests feed its line score and give those extra attention in Part III of this book.

## Line Scores (Composite Scores)

Each branch builds its own job scores from different combinations of subtests. Here are the ones the services publish most clearly.

### Army line scores

These combinations come from the Army's official recruiting site. Each is the sum of the standard scores listed.

| Line score | Subtests | Example job from GoArmy.com |
|---|---|---|
| **GT** – General Technical | VE + AR | Stryker Systems Maintainer (91S) |
| **CL** – Clerical | VE + AR + MK | Human Resources Specialist (42A) |
| **CO** – Combat | VE + AS + MC | Infantryman (11B) |
| **EL** – Electronics | GS + AR + MK + EI | Radar Repairer (94M) |
| **FA** – Field Artillery | AR + MK + MC | Field Artillery Recruit (13U) |
| **GM** – General Maintenance | GS + AS + MK + EI | Parachute Rigger (92R) |
| **MM** – Mechanical Maintenance | AS + MC + EI | Self-Propelled Artillery Systems Mechanic (91P) |
| **OF** – Operators & Food | VE + AS + MC | Culinary Specialist (92G) |
| **SC** – Surveillance & Communications | VE + AR + AS + MC | Electronic Warfare Specialist (17E) |
| **ST** – Skilled Technical | GS + VE + MK + MC | Ammunition Specialist (89B) |

::: tip The famous "GT 110"
The Army's **GT** score (VE + AR) is one of the most important numbers in the Army. A GT of **110** is a common requirement for programs such as Officer Candidate School, Warrant Officer programs, and Special Forces. Because GT is built from verbal and arithmetic skills, studying for your AFQT raises your GT at the same time.
:::

### Air Force and Space Force (MAGE)

The Air Force and Space Force use four composites known as **MAGE**. Each is reported as a percentile from 1 to 99. According to the Air Force's official site:

| Composite | Built from |
|---|---|
| **M** – Mechanical | AR, AS, MC, and VE |
| **A** – Administrative | MK and VE |
| **G** – General | AR and VE |
| **E** – Electronics | AR, EI, GS, and MK |

### Marine Corps

The Marine Corps uses four main line scores: **GT** (General Technical), **EL** (Electronics), **MM** (Mechanical Maintenance), and **CL** (Clerical). The Marine GT is commonly published as **VE + AR + MC** (scaled) — the Marines are known for including Mechanical Comprehension in GT. EL is commonly published as GS + AR + MK + EI, and CL as VE + AR + MK. MM leans on the mechanical and technical subtests.

### Navy and Coast Guard

The Navy and Coast Guard set a separate requirement for each rating (job), written as a sum of subtests. Typical examples are **VE + AR** (general and administrative jobs), **AR + MK + EI + GS** (electronics and technical jobs), **VE + MK + GS** (medical jobs), and combinations that include **MC, AS,** or **AO** (mechanical and operations jobs). Your recruiter can show you the exact requirement for each job you are interested in.

### Which subtests matter for which kinds of jobs

| If you want jobs in... | Focus extra study on... |
|---|---|
| Administration, intelligence, logistics, communications | **WK, PC, AR, MK** |
| Electronics, IT, cyber, avionics, radar | **GS, AR, MK, EI** |
| Mechanics, engineering, aircraft and vehicle maintenance | **AS, MC, EI**, plus GS and AR |
| Medical and health care | **GS, WK, PC, MK** |
| Combat arms | **WK, PC, AS, MC** (Army CO), plus AR |
| Navy operations and some technical ratings | Add **AO** to your practice |

## How Retesting Works

The official retest policy is the same whether your first test was a student test or an enlistment test:

1. After your **first** test, you must wait **one calendar month** before retesting.
2. After your **first retest**, you must wait another **one calendar month**.
3. After that, you must wait **six months** between retests.

"One calendar month" means the same date the next month: if you retested on March 20, your next retest can be on or after April 20.

Other rules worth knowing:

- If your AFQT jumps **20 or more points within six months**, you will be asked to take a **confirmation test** to verify the gain. You can take it right away.
- Scores can be used for enlistment for **two years** from the test date.
- Your **most recent** valid score is your score of record — **not your highest**. A lower retest score replaces a higher earlier score.
- The **PiCAT** can be taken only once, and only by someone who has never taken the ASVAB.

::: strategy Retest only when you are ready
Because your newest score counts, never retest "just to see." Retest when your practice-test results are clearly higher than your last real score.
:::

## "How Many Questions Do I Need Right?"

There is no official chart that turns "number correct" into an AFQT. The ASVAB uses a scoring method called **Item Response Theory**, which considers how difficult each question was, how well it separates stronger and weaker test-takers, and how likely someone is to guess it. On the computer test, two people who get the same number right can earn different scores if one of them answered harder questions.

What you *can* do is measure your readiness with the practice tests in this book:

| Your results on the AFQT subtests of the practice tests | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Below about 60% correct | Keep building basics. Focus on vocabulary and the Math Foundations chapter. |
| About 60–75% correct | Solid progress. Drill your weakest problem types and keep timing yourself. |
| About 80% correct or better, consistently | A good sign you are in range for a **70+**. |
| About 90% correct or better, consistently | You are positioned to push into the **80s and 90s**. |

These ranges are a rough rule of thumb, **not** an official conversion. Real test questions and conditions differ, and the computer test adjusts its difficulty to you. Use the numbers to track your progress, and keep pushing your accuracy higher.

## Chapter Summary

- Each subtest gives a **standard score** (average 50).
- **AFQT = 2 × VE + AR + MK**, reported as a percentile from 1 to 99. Verbal is about **half**.
- **70–80 = Category II**, well above every branch's minimum.
- **Line scores** decide jobs; VE and AR appear in most of them.
- Retest waits: **1 month, 1 month, then 6 months**. Your **most recent** score counts. Scores last **2 years**.
