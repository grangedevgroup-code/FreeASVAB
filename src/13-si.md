# Shop Information {data-sub="Tools, fasteners, materials, and shop practices — what each tool is for and how to use it safely"}

Shop Information (SI) tests your knowledge of tools, fasteners, materials, and common shop and construction practices. On the computer test you get **10 questions in 6 minutes** — about 36 seconds each. Your Shop and Auto results are combined into one **AS** score.

Many SI questions show a **picture of a tool** and ask what it is or what it is used for. Others ask which tool or fastener is best for a job. Study the pictures and tables in this chapter until you can name each tool on sight.

## Measuring and Layout Tools

| Tool | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Tape measure / steel rule / folding rule** | Measuring length |
| **Combination square** | Checking and marking 90° and 45° angles; has a sliding head and often a small level |
| **Try square** | Checking and marking 90° angles on small work |
| **Framing (carpenter's) square** | A large L-shaped square for laying out rafters and stairs and checking corners |
| **Sliding T-bevel** | Copying and transferring any angle |
| **Level** (spirit level) | Checking whether a surface is **level** (horizontal) or **plumb** (vertical), using bubble vials |
| **Plumb bob** | A pointed weight on a string that shows **true vertical** |
| **Chalk line** | Snapping long, straight lines |
| **Calipers** (inside, outside, vernier, dial) | Measuring diameters and thicknesses precisely |
| **Micrometer** | Very precise measurements, to a thousandth of an inch or finer |
| **Feeler gauge** | Measuring **small gaps**, such as spark plug gaps and valve clearances |
| **Marking gauge** | Scribing a line parallel to the edge of a board |
| **Scriber or scratch awl** | Scratching layout lines on metal or wood |
| **Center punch** | Making a small dent so a drill bit starts in the right spot on metal |
| **Dividers / compass** | Drawing circles and transferring measurements |
| **Protractor** | Measuring angles |
{.compact}

### Reading a ruler or tape measure

Most rulers divide each inch into **16 equal parts**. The longer the mark, the bigger the fraction.

{{fig:si-ruler|One inch divided into sixteenths}}

::: tip Counting sixteenths
To read a mark, count the sixteenths and simplify: 8 sixteenths = [[1/2]] inch, 4 sixteenths = [[1/4]] inch, 12 sixteenths = [[3/4]] inch, 6 sixteenths = [[3/8]] inch.
:::

## Striking Tools

| Tool | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Claw hammer** | Driving and pulling nails (a **curved** claw is best for pulling nails; a straight **rip** claw is for prying) |
| **Ball-peen hammer** | **Metalworking**: shaping metal, setting **rivets**, and striking punches and cold chisels |
| **Sledgehammer** | Heavy work: demolition, driving stakes |
| **Mallet** (wood, rubber, or rawhide) | Striking wood chisels or tapping parts without damaging them |
| **Dead-blow hammer** | Filled with shot so it does not bounce back |
| **Nail set** | Driving the head of a finishing nail just below the surface |
| **Punches** (center, pin, drift) | Marking metal, driving out pins, aligning holes |
{.compact}

## Saws

| Saw | Best for |
|---|---|
| **Crosscut saw** | Cutting **across** the grain of wood (teeth shaped like knife points) |
| **Ripsaw** | Cutting **with** (along) the grain (teeth shaped like little chisels) |
| **Backsaw** | Fine, straight cuts; has a stiff spine and is used with a **miter box** for accurate angles |
| **Coping saw** | Cutting **curves** and detailed shapes in thin wood; thin blade in a U-shaped frame |
| **Keyhole (compass) saw / drywall saw** | Starting a cut in the middle of a panel, such as a hole for a pipe or electrical box |
| **Hacksaw** | Cutting **metal** (and plastic pipe); install the blade with the teeth pointing **away from the handle**, because it cuts on the **push** stroke |
| **Circular saw** | Power saw for straight cuts in lumber and plywood |
| **Jigsaw (saber saw)** | Power saw for **curves** |
| **Reciprocating saw** | Demolition and rough cutting |
| **Miter saw (chop saw)** | Accurate crosscuts at set angles |
| **Table saw** | Rip cuts and crosscuts using a fence or miter gauge |
| **Band saw / scroll saw** | Curves (the scroll saw for very fine, intricate curves) |
{.compact}

::: memory Crosscut vs. rip
A **cross**cut saw goes **across** the grain. A **rip**saw **rips** along the grain, the way you would rip a piece of paper along its length.
:::

## Other Cutting and Shaping Tools

- **Wood chisel:** removes wood and cuts recesses (mortises); strike it with a mallet.
- **Cold chisel:** cuts **cold metal** — rivets, bolts, and sheet metal; strike it with a ball-peen hammer.
- **Plane:** shaves thin layers off wood to smooth or straighten it (block plane, jack plane, smoothing plane).
- **File:** smooths and shapes **metal**; it cuts on the **push** stroke. Clean it with a **file card**. **Single-cut** files have one set of parallel teeth; **double-cut** files have crisscrossing teeth and remove material faster.
- **Rasp:** a very coarse file for **wood**.
- **Tin snips (aviation snips):** cut **sheet metal**.
- **Bolt cutters:** cut bolts, chain, and heavy wire.
- **Utility knife:** cuts drywall, cardboard, and carpet.
- **Pipe or tubing cutter:** cuts copper or steel pipe with a cutting wheel; a **reamer** then removes the burr inside.
- **Sandpaper:** the **lower the grit number, the coarser** the paper. 60 is coarse, 120 is medium, 220 is fine. Sand with the grain and move from coarse to fine.

## Drilling and Boring

| Tool or bit | Use |
|---|---|
| **Twist drill bit** | The most common bit, for metal, wood, and plastic |
| **Spade (paddle) bit** | Fast, wide holes in wood |
| **Auger bit** | Deep holes in wood; has a screw tip that pulls it in |
| **Forstner bit** | Clean, flat-bottomed holes in wood |
| **Hole saw** | Large-diameter holes (for door locks, pipes) |
| **Masonry bit** | Carbide-tipped, for concrete and brick (often used with a **hammer drill**) |
| **Countersink** | Cuts a **cone-shaped** recess so a **flat-head** screw sits **flush** with the surface |
| **Counterbore** | Cuts a flat-bottomed recess so a screw or bolt head sits **below** the surface |
| **Brace and bit** | Hand-powered drilling of large holes in wood |
| **Drill press** | Stationary drill for straight, accurate holes |
| **Tap** | Cuts **internal** threads (inside a hole) |
| **Die** | Cuts **external** threads (on a rod or bolt) |

## Wrenches, Screwdrivers, and Pliers

### Wrenches

| Wrench | Features and use |
|---|---|
| **Open-end** | U-shaped opening; slides onto a nut from the side; can slip and round off nuts |
| **Box-end** | A closed ring that grips all sides of the nut; much less likely to slip |
| **Combination** | Open end on one side, box end on the other (same size) |
| **Adjustable (crescent)** | Adjustable jaw fits many sizes; pull so the force is on the **fixed** jaw |
| **Socket wrench with ratchet** | Fast turning; sockets come in **SAE** (inch) and **metric** (millimeter) sizes and in 6-point and 12-point styles |
| **Torque wrench** | Tightens bolts to an **exact specified tightness** (in foot-pounds or newton-meters), such as lug nuts and cylinder head bolts |
| **Pipe wrench** | Grips **round pipe** with serrated jaws (it leaves marks); use two — one to hold and one to turn |
| **Allen (hex) key** | Turns screws with a six-sided socket in the head |
| **Flare-nut (line) wrench** | Brake and fuel line fittings; grips more of the nut than an open-end wrench |
| **Lug wrench** | Removes the lug nuts on car wheels |
{.compact}

::: memory Righty-tighty, lefty-loosey
Standard (right-hand) threads **tighten clockwise** and **loosen counterclockwise**.
:::

### Screwdrivers

{{fig:si-drives|Common screw drive types}}

Match the screwdriver to the screw. A tip that is too small or the wrong type will **strip** (cam out of) the screw head. An **offset screwdriver** reaches screws in tight spaces.

### Pliers

| Pliers | Use |
|---|---|
| **Slip-joint** | General gripping; the pivot has two positions for small or large objects |
| **Tongue-and-groove (water pump, Channellock)** | Adjustable jaws that open wide; plumbing and large nuts |
| **Needle-nose (long-nose)** | Small parts, tight spaces, bending wire |
| **Locking (Vise-Grip)** | Clamp onto work and **lock** in place |
| **Diagonal cutters (side cutters)** | Cutting wire close to a surface |
| **Lineman's pliers** | Heavy-duty gripping, twisting, and cutting of electrical wire |
| **Wire strippers** | Removing insulation from wire |
{.compact}

::: trap Pliers are not wrenches
Using pliers on nuts and bolts rounds off their corners. Use the correct-size wrench or socket.
:::

## Clamps and Vises

- **Bench vise:** mounted to a workbench; holds work for sawing, filing, and drilling.
- **C-clamp:** a C-shaped frame with a screw; holds pieces together or to a bench.
- **Bar clamp and pipe clamp:** long clamps for gluing wide panels.
- **Spring clamp:** quick, light clamping.
- **Hand screw:** wooden jaws that will not mar wood.
- **Miter or corner clamp:** holds two pieces at a right angle.

## Fasteners

{{fig:si-fasteners|Common fasteners}}

### Nails

| Nail | Features and use |
|---|---|
| **Common nail** | Thick shank, flat head; framing and general construction |
| **Box nail** | Thinner than a common nail, so less likely to split wood |
| **Finishing nail** | Very small head that is **set below the surface** and hidden; trim and cabinets |
| **Brad** | A small, thin finishing nail |
| **Roofing nail** | Short with a **large, flat head** to hold shingles; usually galvanized |
| **Duplex (double-headed) nail** | Two heads, so it can be **pulled out easily**; temporary work such as concrete forms and scaffolding |
| **Masonry nail** | Hardened steel for concrete and brick |
{.compact}

**Nail sizes** use the "penny" system, written **d**. A **2d** nail is 1 inch long, a **6d** is 2 inches, an **8d** is 2½ inches, a **10d** is 3 inches, and a **16d** (a common framing nail) is 3½ inches. The bigger the penny number, the longer the nail.

### Screws, bolts, and nuts

| Fastener | Features and use |
|---|---|
| **Wood screw** | Tapered with coarse threads; grips wood |
| **Sheet-metal screw** | Sharp, threaded all the way; cuts its own threads in thin metal |
| **Machine screw** | Even threads; goes into a nut or a threaded (tapped) hole |
| **Lag screw (lag bolt)** | A heavy screw with a hex head, turned with a wrench into wood |
| **Carriage bolt** | Rounded head with a **square neck** that locks into wood, so the head does not need a wrench |
| **Hex bolt (machine bolt)** | Six-sided head; used with a nut |
| **Toggle bolt / molly (hollow-wall anchor)** | Spread out behind drywall or other hollow walls |
| **Anchor bolt** | Set into concrete to hold down structures |
| **Wing nut** | Has "wings" so it can be tightened by hand |
| **Lock nut** | Has a nylon insert or special shape that resists loosening |
| **Castle nut** | Has slots for a **cotter pin** that keeps it from turning |
| **Flat washer** | Spreads the load and protects the surface |
| **Lock washer** (split or toothed) | Keeps a nut or bolt from loosening |
| **Rivet** | A permanent fastener; a **pop (blind) rivet** is installed from one side with a rivet tool |
{.compact}

**Thread terms:** **pitch** is the distance between threads; **threads per inch (TPI)** counts them. **Coarse** threads assemble quickly; **fine** threads hold better against vibration. Screw heads come in **flat** (countersunk), **round**, **oval**, **pan**, and **hex** styles.

## Wood and Woodworking

- **Hardwoods** come from broad-leaf (deciduous) trees: oak, maple, walnut, cherry, hickory, ash. **Softwoods** come from conifers (needle-bearing trees): pine, fir, spruce, cedar, redwood.
- **Lumber sizes are nominal.** A "2-by-4" actually measures **1½ by 3½ inches** after drying and planing. A 2×6 is 1½ × 5½; a 1×6 is ¾ × 5½; a 4×4 is 3½ × 3½.
- A **board foot** is a piece 1 foot long, 1 foot wide, and 1 inch thick (144 cubic inches).
- **Plywood** is made of thin layers (plies) glued with the grain of each layer at right angles to the next, which makes it strong and resistant to warping. Other sheet goods include **OSB**, **particleboard**, and **MDF**.
- **Pressure-treated** lumber resists rot and insects and is used outdoors and in contact with the ground.

| Joint | Description |
|---|---|
| **Butt joint** | Two pieces simply butted together — the simplest and weakest |
| **Miter joint** | Ends cut at an angle (usually 45°) to form a corner, as in a picture frame |
| **Lap joint** | One piece overlaps the other |
| **Dado** | A groove cut **across** the grain to hold a shelf |
| **Rabbet** | An L-shaped notch cut along the **edge** of a board |
| **Mortise and tenon** | A tongue (tenon) fits into a hole (mortise); very strong, used in furniture |
| **Dovetail** | Interlocking, fan-shaped "tails" and "pins"; very strong, used in drawers |
| **Tongue and groove** | A ridge on one board fits a groove in the next; used in flooring |
{.compact}

**Glues and finishes:** yellow **wood glue** for general woodworking; two-part **epoxy** for strong, gap-filling bonds; **contact cement** for laminates. **Stain** adds color; **varnish, polyurethane, lacquer,** and **shellac** add a protective clear coat.

## Metals and Metalworking

- **Ferrous** metals contain **iron** (steel, cast iron). They are usually magnetic and can **rust**. **Nonferrous** metals contain no iron (aluminum, copper, brass, bronze, lead, zinc).

| Alloy | Made of | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel** | Iron + carbon | Strong; the more carbon, the harder |
| **Stainless steel** | Steel + chromium (often nickel) | Resists rust |
| **Brass** | Copper + **zinc** | Plumbing fittings, ammunition casings |
| **Bronze** | Copper + **tin** | Bearings, statues |
| **Solder** | Tin-based alloy (older solder contained lead) | Low melting point; joins electrical connections and copper pipe |
{.compact}

- **Galvanized** steel has a **zinc** coating that protects it from rust.
- **Properties:** **hardness** (resists scratches and dents), **ductility** (can be stretched into wire), **malleability** (can be hammered into thin sheets), **brittleness** (breaks without bending, like cast iron), and **tensile strength** (resists being pulled apart).
- **Heat treating steel:**
  - **Hardening:** heat it, then cool it **quickly** (quench in water or oil). It becomes hard but brittle.
  - **Tempering:** reheat hardened steel to a lower temperature to reduce brittleness and add toughness.
  - **Annealing:** heat it, then cool it **slowly**. It becomes **softer** and easier to work.
- **Sheet metal gauge:** a **higher** gauge number means **thinner** metal (just like wire).

## Welding, Brazing, and Soldering

| Process | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Stick (arc) welding** | An electric arc between a flux-coated **rod** (electrode) and the metal melts them together |
| **MIG welding** | A **continuously fed wire** electrode plus **shielding gas**; fast and easy to learn |
| **TIG welding** | A **tungsten** electrode that is not consumed, a separate filler rod, and shielding gas (usually argon); precise and clean, good for aluminum and thin metal |
| **Oxyacetylene welding and cutting** | A flame of **oxygen and acetylene** — hot enough to weld and, with a cutting torch, to cut steel |
| **Brazing** | A filler metal (often brass) melts and bonds the pieces **without melting the base metal**; done at a higher temperature than soldering |
| **Soldering** | A low-melting **solder** bonds electrical connections and copper plumbing; **flux** cleans the metal so the solder flows |
{.compact}

::: trap Welding safety
The light from an electric arc can burn your eyes ("arc eye") and skin, so always wear a welding helmet with the correct **shade** lens, gloves, and protective clothing. Weld only with good ventilation, and never weld or cut a container that has held anything flammable.
:::

## Masonry and Concrete

- **Concrete** = Portland **cement** + **sand** + **gravel** (aggregate) + **water**. It hardens by a chemical reaction called **curing** and keeps getting stronger for weeks; keep it moist while it cures.
- **Mortar** = cement + sand + water (and often lime). It bonds bricks and blocks.
- Concrete is very strong in **compression** (being squeezed) but weak in **tension** (being pulled), so it is reinforced with steel **rebar**.
- **Masonry tools:** a **trowel** places and spreads mortar; a **float** smooths concrete; an **edger** rounds edges; a **jointer or groover** cuts control joints so cracks form in straight lines; a **screed** (a straight board) levels wet concrete; a **mason's line** keeps rows of brick straight.

## Plumbing Basics

- **Copper pipe** is joined by soldering ("sweating") with a torch, flux, and solder. **PVC** (white plastic, for drains and cold water) is joined with primer and **solvent cement**. **CPVC** handles hot water. **PEX** is flexible plastic tubing joined with crimp rings.
- **Plumbing tools:** pipe wrench, pipe and tubing cutters, plunger, drain snake (auger), **basin wrench** (for faucet nuts under a sink), and **thread-seal (PTFE/Teflon) tape** for threaded joints.
- The U-shaped **P-trap** under a sink holds water that blocks sewer gases from coming into the room.

## Shop Safety

- Wear **eye protection** at all times; add **hearing protection** for loud tools and a **dust mask** or respirator for dust and fumes.
- No loose clothing or jewelry; tie back long hair.
- **Never remove guards** from power tools.
- **Unplug** a power tool before changing blades or bits.
- **Clamp** small workpieces; never hold them by hand while drilling.
- Keep tools **sharp** — dull tools need more force and slip more easily.
- **Kickback** is when a saw throws the workpiece back toward the operator. Stand to the side of the blade, and use a push stick and the saw's riving knife or splitter.
- Use **GFCI** outlets and **grounded** (three-prong) or **double-insulated** tools.

| Fire class | Fuel | Memory aid |
|---|---|---|
| **Class A** | Ordinary combustibles: wood, paper, cloth | Leaves **A**sh |
| **Class B** | Flammable liquids: gasoline, oil, grease, paint | **B**oils or **B**arrels |
| **Class C** | Energized electrical equipment | **C**urrent |
| **Class D** | Combustible metals (magnesium) | **D**ense metals |
| **Class K** | Cooking oils and fats | **K**itchen |
{.compact}

An "ABC" extinguisher works on classes A, B, and C. **Never use water on an electrical or grease fire.**
